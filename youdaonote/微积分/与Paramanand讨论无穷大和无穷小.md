From your mail I understand that you are of the view that numbers (particularly infinite and infinitesimals) have some sort of physical existence (meaning they have a direct relation to reality). Well its not exactly the case in mathematics. In mathematics a thing exists because we define it to exist. Mathematical systems are ultimately based on a set of axioms (which may or may not be based on our experience with real world) and everyone is free to create his own set of axioms and build a theory of it. Thus natural numbers, integers, rationals, real and complex numbers are all a product of Peano's axioms. They don't have a reality like physical objects but their properties are based on our experiences with real world.



In the same manner the proponents of non-standard analysis choose to build a system using different set of axioms and in their system the infinities and infinitesimals have genuine existence. Another idea that each point of a line can be made to correspond with a unique real number in one to one fashion (bijective) is another  axiom which links the fields of analysis and geometry (and is the foundation on which we have developed analytical or coordinate geometry). There is no object in real world like a geometrical line and the existence of geometrical line is also based on Euclid's axioms.



I see the existence of infinity and infinitesimals as being dependent on the system with which we are working (the standard vs non-standard analysis) and both views are compatible with each other. The results derived from non-standard analysis don't contradict the results derived from conventional analysis and vice versa. But it is a wrong idea to criss-cross concepts from one system to another. Thus there is no existence of infinity or infinitesimals in the real number system and we should not feel that this is some sort of deficiency which the non-standard analysis has sort of handled.



Unfortunately I don't have much knowledge of the non-standard analysis so I can't describe much how the infinities and infinitesimals work in that system and whether they are compatible with your intuitive idea of infinitely big/small numbers.

Hello Zhao,

I do remember you. Thanks for expressing your thoughts so nicely into the attached PDF. Well my thoughts on this topic are as follows.



Let us assume that Homer has to climb a height of 2 feet. And height of his initial footstep is 1 feet. Now when he uses one step to reach a height of 1 feet he feels a bit tired and loses some energy so that the next footstep height will be half the height of previous footstep and this pattern is a feature of the way Homer climbs any height. Also let's suppose that time taken for each step is 5 secs (I have chosen it arbitrarily, the main point is that time taken for each step is constant and independent of the fact that height covered in each step is diminishing). Under these circumstances Homer will never be able to climb a height of 2 feet. But he will be able to climb any height less than 2 feet if we wait for long enough time.



The normal case is that the time taken to cover a distance is roughly proportional to distance covered and then it can be easily seen that any specific distance can be covered by Homer if we wait long enough.



The conventional treatment of analysis is based on the idea that we can only deal with things which are finite. Thus we can add and multiply a finite number of quantities. Any given number is finite. This view was championed by Archimedes and we deal with system of numbers which satisfy Archimedean principle which states that "if x, y are any positive numbers then we can find a positive integer n such that nx > y." Both the rational and real numbers satisfy this principle. Any Archimedean system forbids the existence of numbers which are infinite. The idea of infinity in such Archimedean systems is dealt with in the following manner: given any positive number x there is another positive number y greater than x. Thus there is no end to the supply of numbers as large as we please, but any specific number we choose is finite by nature. This is very smart technique invented by Archimedes by which we deal with infnity.



All the traditional development of analysis (limits, continuity, derivative integral) involve the epsilon, delta argument which is nothing but dealing with infinity in a finite manner. Thus when we write "1/n" tends to "0" as n tends to "infinity" we mean that the value 1/n can be made smaller than any specific epsilon by choosing n bigger than a suitable specific integer depending on epsilon. In this respect I tend to fully agree with the view of Richard Courant expressed in his book "What is Mathematics?".



There is another system which was invented recently called Non-standard analysis which ditches Archimedean principle and instead tries to put the idea of infinity and infinitesimals (championed by Newton, Leibniz) on a rigorous foundation. In this system of analysis we construct a system of Hyperreal numbers which include the reals, the infinitesimals, and the infinity. But I don't find this approach very satisfying. The Archimedean principle is more intuitive and resembles very well with the concept of magnitude as we seen in our daily lives. Thus for example even we have to travel 2000 kms we know that we can cover this distance by our small footsteps in a finite number of steps. There is no infinity in our real world everyday experience.
