Yes, you're absolutely right.
In **Richard Dedekind's** work, particularly the one you're referring to — **"Was sind und was sollen die Zahlen?"** (translated: **"What are numbers and what should they be?"**), available at Project Gutenberg ([PDF link](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/21016/21016-pdf.pdf)) — Dedekind **formulated a foundation for the natural numbers using set-theoretic concepts**. This was a groundbreaking shift toward a formal, logic-based foundation for arithmetic.
### Key points Dedekind made:
- He 
**defined natural numbers as a simply infinite system**, a set $N$ together with:
- A distinguished element (interpreted as zero or one),
- A successor function $\varphi$ that maps each element to its successor.
- He introduced the notion of a 
**chain** (Kette), which helped define the set of natural numbers as the **smallest** set closed under the successor function.
- Most importantly, he demonstrated that 
**natural numbers could be derived purely from set-theoretic and logical constructions**, without relying on intuition or empirical notions.
This was a **major advance in the logicist program** — the effort to reduce all of mathematics to logic and set theory. His approach directly influenced later foundational work by Peano, Frege, and Russell.
So yes — Dedekind showed that the concept of natural numbers **can be constructed purely from set-theoretic principles**, making arithmetic **a branch of logic**.
